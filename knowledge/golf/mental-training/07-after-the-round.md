# After the Round

The round is over. What you do with the next ten minutes decides what it was worth.

[BEAT]

First, settle for a minute — wherever this finds you, walking, driving, or
somewhere quiet. If your eyes can close, let them. If you are moving, keep them
open and soft.

In, through your nose.

[PAUSE]

And out, through your mouth, longer than you took it in.

[PAUSE]

Again. In.

[PAUSE]

And out, longer.

[PAUSE — LONG]

[BEAT]

There are two ways to waste a round after it ends, and most golfers alternate
between them. The first is to stew — take it home, replay the misses at dinner, let
a number follow you around for two days. The second is to slam the trunk and never
think about it again, which throws away everything the round just taught you. Same
result either way: you paid for eighteen holes of information and kept none of it.

The tool is a post-round assessment with an expiration date. Ten minutes. Honest,
specific, and then closed.

And hear what it is not: it is not adding up your score and feeling something about
it. The questions that matter are mental-game questions, because that is where the
strokes actually went. Where did my focus go, and what pulled it? After the worst
hole, what did my reaction cost — the hole, or the hole plus the next three? It is
almost never the double that ruins a card. It is the reaction to the double. Did I
trust the swing I brought, or did I steer it around the course? Did I run the
routine on every shot, or did frustration start rushing it on the greens? And all
day — was I aiming at what I wanted, or spending half the holes aiming at what I
was trying to avoid?

One caution while you grade: the routine's job was never to be beautiful. Do not
score yourself on choreography. Score yourself on commitment.

Out of those ten minutes comes one thing: the lesson. One sentence. Not five
sentences — the one thing you will actually carry to the next round. Write it where
you keep your golf notes. Learn the lesson, and leave the event behind. The lesson
travels with you. The event does not get a seat in the car.

[BEAT]

Then the switch, and make it physical, because the body ends things the mind will
keep negotiating. The spikes come off — and golf comes off with them. That is the
whole ritual. Shoes in the trunk, game in the trunk. Walk into the house as the
other people in your life know you: the spouse, the parent, the friend. They did
not see a number walk in. Nobody who cares about you sees one.

That separation is not soft. It is structural, and it protects both sides. The
person is not the athlete: your score is a fact about one afternoon of golf, not a
fact about you. Golfers who cannot put the game down do not get more from it — they
get less, because the stewing rehearses failure, and because a life with no room in
it for anything else gives the next first tee far too much weight. You play better
when the round is allowed to end.

[BEAT — HOLD]

Now run it, on the last round you actually played. Bring it up — the course, the
day, the number. Walk the questions. Where did the focus go? What did the worst
reaction cost? Steering or trusting? Rushed routines? Aiming at wants, or at
avoids?

[PAUSE — LONG]

Now the lesson. One sentence, and say it like you are writing it down.

[PAUSE — LONG]

And now the switch. Spikes off. Feel the game come off with them, and the rest of
your life come back on. The lesson stays. The event is over.

[PAUSE — LONG]

[BEAT — HOLD]

The one thing: ten honest minutes, one written lesson — and when the spikes come
off, golf comes off with them.
