# Between Shots

How do you stay focused for four and a half hours of golf?

You don't. That is the answer, and this session is about what to do instead.

[BEAT]

First, settle for a minute — wherever this finds you, walking, warming up, or
somewhere quiet. If your eyes can close, let them. If you are moving, keep them open
and soft.

In, through your nose.

[PAUSE]

And out, through your mouth, longer than you took it in.

[PAUSE]

Again. In.

[PAUSE]

And out, longer.

[PAUSE — LONG]

[BEAT]

Run the math on a round. Four hours and change, seventy-some swings, and your club
is actually moving for under two minutes of it. Golf is unlike any other sport this
way — slow, self-paced, no opponent forcing your attention anywhere, and enormous
gaps with nothing to do but think. Whatever your mind does in those gaps is the
state you arrive at the next ball with. Rounds are won and lost in the gaps.

Trying to grind — locked in from the first tee to the last putt — is the wrong
answer, and not because you are weak. Nobody can concentrate for four hours.
Concentration is a consumable, and grinding burns through it by the twelfth hole,
exactly when you need it most. The real skill is an ebb and a flow: fully dialed in
for about thirty seconds a shot, genuinely off duty in between. That is roughly
fifteen minutes of real focus spread across a round, and anyone can afford that.

Here is the mechanism worth knowing. Attention is a spotlight with two dials.
Direction — outside at the world, or inside at your own thoughts. And width — broad,
taking everything in, or narrow, locked on one thing. Broad and outside is the
walking setting: wind, terrain, what the last green told you. Narrow and outside is
the over-the-ball setting: one target, one cue. The expensive setting is narrow and
inside — replaying the last shot, doing arithmetic on the score, running commentary
on your own swing. It keeps the stress chemistry switched on through the exact
minutes your system was supposed to be recovering, and it points you at the one
place on the course with no information in it.

[BEAT]

So here are the tools.

Give focus a trigger. The homework — yardage, lie, wind — you can do in your sleep,
so let it be casual. What you cannot do in your sleep is see the shot. So pick a
physical moment that clicks you in — pulling the club from the bag works — and from
that moment you are all golf for thirty seconds: picture, last look, cue, swing.
After the shot, click out just as deliberately and hand the round back to the walk.

Between shots, be off duty on purpose. Talk to your group. Notice the day. Enjoy
it — that is not lost focus, that is how focus is budgeted. One boundary: the
off-duty channel is the course and the company, not your life. Work, family,
the phone — those stay in the car. You are in the role of the golfer until the last
putt drops.

And when you catch yourself narrow and inside — replaying, tallying — do not argue
with the thought. Attention never switches off; it only relocates. Eyes up, find
something at distance, and let your eyes rest there until the spotlight follows.
The eyes lead. The mind follows. It is faster than willpower, every time.

[BEAT — HOLD]

Now train it, because the reset only works if it is practiced. Bring up a shot from
a recent round that stung — one you caught yourself replaying. Let the replay
actually start. Feel the pull inward.

[PAUSE — LONG]

Now: eyes up. Something at distance. Let the eyes rest there and feel the spotlight
swing outside. Broad. Wind, ground, day.

[PAUSE — LONG]

Now reach for the club. Feel the click — thirty seconds, all golf. Picture, last
look, cue. Then the shot is gone, and you hand the round back to the walk.

[PAUSE — LONG]

[BEAT — HOLD]

The one thing: golf is not four hours of focus. It is thirty seconds at a time,
clicked in at the club, clicked out after the shot — and eyes up the moment you
catch yourself inside.
