# Between Rounds

Where does a good practice session actually go?

[BEAT]

First, settle for a minute — wherever this finds you, walking, driving, or
somewhere quiet. If your eyes can close, let them. If you are moving, keep them
open and soft.

In, through your nose.

[PAUSE]

And out, through your mouth, longer than you took it in.

[PAUSE]

Again. In.

[PAUSE]

And out, longer.

[PAUSE — LONG]

[BEAT]

The answer is: one place only. Your brain. Muscles store nothing — they have no
memory to store with. Every feel you find on the range, every fix, every pured
iron exists afterward as a memory or it does not exist at all. And the brain does
its permanent filing at one time only: while you sleep.

Now the problem. The brain's default filing favors the negative — that is survival
wiring, older than golf. Most of any day is simply discarded before you sleep, and
what survives without help is the argument, the worry, the stress spike — not the
swing feel you found at eleven in the morning. Follow a great practice with a loud,
stressful afternoon and the afternoon is what gets written. The practice is gone,
and next round you will stand on the range trying to remember what it was you had.
A practice you cannot retrieve on the course never happened.

So the skill of this session is not practicing more. It is making practice stick,
and it has four parts.

First, close the session on paper. The moment practice ends, record the keys while
you still have them — two or three lines where you keep your golf notes, or a short
video talking through the feel. What you found. What it felt like. What to do first
next time. Thirty seconds of writing protects three hours of work.

Second, guard the hour after. Filing begins as soon as you stop, and whatever
happens next competes with the practice for storage. You cannot control your whole
day — but the fight that can wait, the stressful call that can wait, let them wait
an hour.

Third, re-shelve before you sleep. In the last half hour of the day, bring the keys
back to the top of the pile: read the note, replay the feel in your head, watch the
video. What is on top when you fall asleep is what the brain writes. This is also
the honest version of ending the day on your best swings instead of your worst
ones — you are choosing what gets kept.

Fourth, balance the ledger. Four-one-one: name four specific good things from this
day, one negative thing, and the one thing you will do about the negative tomorrow.
This is not gratitude practice — it is countermanding the negative default, so the
day files clean and the mind quiets enough to sleep. Because all of the above runs
on sleep. It is the cheapest performance enhancer in golf: protect a consistent
window, and no swing change will ever give you back what a short night takes.

[BEAT — HOLD]

Now run it, on your most recent practice. Bring the session up. Find the one feel
worth keeping — the best thing you left with.

[PAUSE — LONG]

Say the note you would write: what you found, what it felt like, what to do first
next time. Plain words, like you are writing it down.

[PAUSE — LONG]

Now today. Four specific good things from this day.

[PAUSE — LONG]

One negative. And the one thing you will do about it tomorrow.

[PAUSE — LONG]

[BEAT — HOLD]

The one thing: practice only exists if you can retrieve it — write the keys down,
keep the next hour calm, put them back on top before you sleep, and file the day
four-one-one.
